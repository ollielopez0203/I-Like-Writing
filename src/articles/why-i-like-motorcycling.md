I think a lot, maybe even all of what I'm going to say here might be analogous to your own experiences, but I can only speak from my experience. The fact that it is relatable and transferable is also pretty cool, and why I think it's worth sharing.

Over the last few years, I've come to love motorcycling. As a mode of transport, sure. It's cheap and convenient. It's also pop culture, heritage, danger and rebellion. It's Akira, Isle of Man TT and Top Gun. There is an allure to motorcycling that captivates me, and maybe it captivates you too. Growing up, my mum often said something to the effect of, 'no matter what you do in life, please don't ride a motorcycle.' She had me tactically promise her that I wouldn't, probably hundreds of times before I was ever actually exposed to one. Eventually, I found myself learning about it. How to ride, shift gears, use the clutch, rear brake and front brake. It feels foolish to start anything; you're constantly reminded that you are not good and that you should probably give up to stay cool. But I stuck with it, became a proudly mediocre weekend rider and was introduced to the rich and complex world of riding stuff on 2 wheels.

There is something to be said here about the counter-culture and social rebellion that motorcycling represents, particularly in western media. It's not the whole picture, but it certainly plays a role in its allure. At a time where I knew nothing of my path in life, it was a middle finger to logic and society. It was also a middle finger to rules and expectations. Total, unbridled freedom. It was an expression of self, and catharsis for frustration and teenage angst. In my small world it meant ripping the throttle on a 20 horsepower lawnmower, scraping pegs on roundabouts and going 10km over the limit to stick it to the man. I still love that, but I think it's evolved into something more than self expression and catharsis.

In a vacuum, motorcycling is an offensive creation, a slap in the face to God and a blatant insult to the gift and sanctity of human life. I find beauty and humour in that - it's this relentless pursuit of thrill and speed that gives it this childish, playful quality. There are real stakes with serious, irreversible consequences, but you do it because it's fun. We spend millions on R&D and trickle down technology that gives your balding middle aged uncle enough braking power to stop a freight train and your pissant nephew enough power to vaporise a small kangaroo. To ride means accepting that the miracle that is your life, utterly invaluable and infinite, can end because of forces beyond your control.
