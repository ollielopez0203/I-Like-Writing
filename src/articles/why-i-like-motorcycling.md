Over the last few years, I've come to love motorcycling. As a mode of transport, sure. It's cheap and convenient. It's also pop culture, heritage, danger and rebellion. It's Akira, Isle of Man TT and Top Gun. There is an allure to motorcycling that captivates me, and maybe it captivates you too. Growing up, my mum often said something to the effect of, 'no matter what you do in life, please don't ride a motorcycle.' She had me tactically promise her that I wouldn't, probably hundreds of times before I even turned 18. Much to her dismay, I eventually found myself learning how to ride, shift gears, use the clutch, rear brake and front brake. It feels foolish to start anything; you're constantly reminded that you are not good and that you should probably give up to stay cool. But I stuck with it; became a proudly mediocre weekend warrior, and was introduced to the rich and complex world of riding stuff on 2 wheels.

There is something to be said about the counter-culture and social rebellion that motorcycling represents, particularly in western media. It's not the whole picture, but it certainly plays a role in its allure. There is an image associated with lawlessness, freedom and speed. At a time where I knew nothing of my path in life, it was a middle finger to logic and society. And sorry mum, it was also a middle finger to family values and expectations. When everyone tells you no, it’s a satisfying form of protest to say yes. It was an expression of self, free from societal pressures and a catharsis for frustration and teenage angst. In my small world, it meant ripping the throttle on a 20 horsepower lawnmower, scraping pegs on roundabouts and going 10km over the limit to stick it to the man. It’s obnoxiously loud and flashy, but its also silly. I still love that, but spite alone is not everything.

Motorcycling to me is two worlds: one is practicality and global context; a useful product that serves the purpose of industrialisation and the movement of goods and people. The other is an obscene creation; a slap in the face of God and a blatant insult to the gift and sanctity of human life. I find beauty and humour in that - it's this relentless pursuit of thrill and speed that gives it this childish, playful quality. There are real stakes with serious, irreversible consequences, but you do it anyway because it's *fun*. Maybe it reminds you of being a kid again, playing with something that you know you shouldn't be playing with. It shines a light on the absurdity of life and our apparent values. We invest billions in the protection and preservation of human life, yet the Isle of Man TT regularly kills 2-3 riders per year. Every year. Since 1907. Our apparent values tell us that we should stop. Self preservation tells us that we should stop. But we don't. We spend millions on R&D and trickle down technology to give your balding uncle enough braking power to stop a freight train, and your neighborhood punk enough power to vaporise a kangaroo. It seems completely unnecessary and even nonsensical when you try to define it using logic. But, it is that very reality that brings me joy and perspective, because when your world feels heavy and serious, what a privilege it is to swing a leg over a lime green Japanese crotch rocket, that was precision engineered to make you go fast.

To ride means accepting that the miracle that is your life, utterly invaluable and infinite, can end because of forces beyond your control. Of course, this is always true - you are always at the mercy of cosmic forces that can kill you at any time. Riding just makes you aware of that; you are aware that any wrong input can have disastrous consequences. It’s a feeling of control, or at least a very good imitation of control. Your fate is in your hands, and you are responsible for everything in that moment. Total presence and focus. It just feels real, and in this era of uncertainty, that feels important.

Maybe you have something in your life that makes you feel this way. At the end of the day, I don't think it's actually about motorcycling. It's just about doing something, and I love it because I can project my worldview and feelings onto it. One could argue that any of our hobbies are just a manifestation of the same thing. As Richard Feynman once said, 'nearly anything is interesting if you go into it deeply enough.'
