It is not much of a stretch to say that the average English speaker does not appreciate their luck of the draw. I mean a few things by this. 

There is, of course, the fact that the English language is the global language. More opportunities, more information, and more access is afforded to those who speak it. Indeed, Spanish, Chinese, and a few other such languages are widespread, on account of the sizable populations who speak them. These are primary languages to be sure; but there is only one understood lingua franca, and that is English. So this is my first meaning when I say that English speakers have it distinctively well. If you meet someone in a foreign country, it is more likely than not that English, however broken, can facilitate some sort of exchange. At the very least, the universal "hello" alleviates much of that initial uncertainty. Imagine how amazing that is, to have your own language as the medium of communication that everyone, no matter creed or nation, accepts (or rather is forced to accept). Almost like a global currency of its own – a spoken form of dollar – which itself, ironically enough, belongs to the English speakers. 

The average English speaker is unaware of this, just as the average person is unaware of just how lucky they are to have all appendages at their disposal. The works of much smarter people than I have already articulated just how this privilege, amongst many others, belongs to the Western World at the goodwill, and the expense, of everyone else. But that is another discussion entirely.

Just so we understand each other, I generally don't see the ignorance of English speakers as a nefarious one, and any one speaker of English would more often than not be sensible enough to understand their fortune if we were to discuss the matter. But that does not mean that the unconscious day-to-day ignorance of speaking the world's lingua franca has not led to harms.

Herein lies my next few meanings. They overlap, and so I will speak of them in such a way.

There is an unconscious arrogance that forms out of the possession of a privilege that many do not have, in the minds of those who have it. And that is precisely the worst part of it: that its possession is not felt, and is unlikely to be realised, until it has been taken away. English speakers such as myself have this same arrogance about their mindset, and since the global predominance of our mother tongue has yet to be dethroned, that arrogance will remain for the foreseeable future. The benefits that speaking English provides cannot be realised by its speakers without an active effort being made to understand that fact. And that of itself is a trying task if you don't even realise that you have special access and benefits in the first place. 

Ok, so we understand that speaking English is a privilege, and a privilege undetectable to its inheritors at that. And from this privilege, there is arrogance that forms. And this arrogance is unconscious. But why do I call it arrogance, and not mere ignorance? 

It is arrogance because it frames one's thinking in such a way that they see themselves as superior to others. Not outrightly so, however, hence the unconsciousness of it. An English speaker does not (for the most part) consciously view non-English speakers as lesser than. They do not sit there thinking, "this person is thick as eight planks" as they labour to understand the broken sentences of an earnest non-native speaker. But, being the cognitive misers that we are, our brains make simple associations to lessen their load. When we communicate, we see the vocabulary and communicative skills used to express a thought as a faithful mirror of the content and complexity of that thought. It is an easy thing to do, you see. One hears fluency, and assumes fluent thought. Equally, one hears stunted sentences, and assumes the underlying thought is equally stunted. It is innocent slippage, in many ways. But it is damaging.

One may be an expert on the discussed matter, but their input cannot survive the translation into English, and so they are judged anyway. By a monolingual, no less! Someone might rebuff this claim of mine, and that is fair enough. You are free to draw your own conclusions, but this is what I have seen. I have experienced it first-hand from the Americans, and certainly many Australians and Brits, who love so much the novelty of making fun of others' accents. I have heard the choruses of "Herro!" chimed by ensembles of youngsters on the train into the city who giggle earnestly at how funny they are, the Asian woman who passed them safely out of earshot. It is the same behaviour as that of those who mimic people with special needs. It is the mockery of some perceived deficit in someone else.

This opens up so many conversations, all of which have been thoroughly examined. Discussions of who gets access to knowledge, what knowledge is taken seriously, attitudes between cultures, Eurocentrism, Western-centrism, etc.: they all get brought to the forefront. The critical theorists and post-colonialists have done their best work to break this down, so it would be useless for me – a 21-year-old moron – to even try. 

I think this matter needs more discussion today, particularly given the level of anti-immigration sentiment that is doing the rounds. The prestige of speaking English as a first language, and the arrogance that I have found it produces, shows just how contradictory the topic has become. It is said that immigrants do not make an effort in the slightest to assimilate into Western culture, and one of the classic proofs drawn on is the apparent lack of willingness to switch entirely over to English as their primary language. What an absolute farce. I am more than willing to risk that these same white people, with their dirty utterances, make little to no effort when abroad to speak the local language. I have seen it first-hand. And whilst this is not the case absolutely, those who do make an effort I don't doubt are more compassionate and reasonable in their attitudes. 

Having travelled the world, and lived in most corners of it, I do think I have met a very, let's say 'vibrant', array of peoples. And what I have found is that the English speakers of the world (oft-unconsciously) expect others to meet them in their language, and not the other way. Similarly, non-native English speakers do not blink at this double standard. In this sense, both sides uphold the prestige of the language. The arrogance, albeit unconsciously, is provided its stage just as much from those who suffer it as much as those who possess it.

This would be a rather sad essay if I had nowhere to go from here. To be sure, this is unfortunately the way of things at the moment. English speakers have a unique privilege, amongst the many others they have inherited as people of the Western World. In inheriting this privilege, it is critical that we become aware of it. 

No one, I expect, will be reading this essay, and so I will not be making some grand call to arms for the youngsters of the world to widen their eyes; to '开阔眼界' and realise just how good they have it. But I think it should be said that we need to do a better job of meeting the rest of the world in their language when we leave the shores of Australia, the UK, the US, and voyage into the realms of our neighbours. 

It is a warming sight to see, and an elating experience when you stumble to order food in China, or sing karaoke in a Polish karaoke bar, to the cheers and encouragement of locals. They don't judge you implicitly as a half-wit, as someone whose broken sentences somehow match broken thoughts. They understand you are trying, because they have to do it all the time: for more often than not they will be confronted with the horrifying experience of having to speak English to someone who cannot match them in their native tongue.
